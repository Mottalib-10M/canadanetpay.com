Average salary in Canada 2026: the median and the spread

Average and median salaries in Canada based on Statistics Canada data. Earnings by province, industry, age group, and gender with take-home pay breakdowns.

By Radif Partners, passionate about personal finance, Éditeur de calculateurs et de guides pratiques

Average weekly earnings in Canada are published monthly by Statistics Canada from the Survey of Employment, Payrolls and Hours, and the figure that circulates in the press is usually that series multiplied out to an annual amount. Three cautions apply before comparing a salary to it. The average includes part-time employment, which pulls the weekly figure down without affecting the hourly one. It includes overtime and bonuses, so the base-pay comparison is with a different series. And the provincial spread is wide, with resource-producing provinces well above the national figure, so a national average describes no particular labour market. The guide gives the national figure, the provincial breakdown and the series that excludes overtime and bonuses, together with the retrieval date, since the series is revised as later returns arrive and a figure without a date cannot be checked against the current release.

Average and Median Salary in Canada

According to Statistics Canada, the average annual salary for full-time employees in Canada is approximately $64,850 as of 2025. However, the average can be misleading because high earners pull the figure up. The median salary -- the point at which half of workers earn more and half earn less -- is approximately $40,500 per year. This gap between the average and median highlights the income inequality in the Canadian labour market.

Understanding where your salary falls relative to these benchmarks helps with career planning, salary negotiations, and financial decisions. These figures are based on the Labour Force Survey and the Canadian Income Survey published by Statistics Canada.

Average Salary by Age Group

Earnings in Canada follow a typical age-earnings profile: they rise steeply from ages 25 to 44 as workers gain experience and qualifications, plateau between ages 45 and 54, and then decline slightly approaching retirement age. Workers aged 25 to 34 earn significantly more than those 15 to 24, reflecting the transition from entry-level positions to established careers.

Average Salary by Gender

The gender wage gap in Canada has narrowed over time but remains significant. As of 2025, women working full-time earn approximately 87 cents for every dollar earned by men in comparable roles. The gap varies by industry, occupation, and province. Factors contributing to the gap include occupational segregation, differences in hours worked, career interruptions, and underrepresentation in higher-paying industries such as mining, technology, and finance.

How Average Salary Translates to Take-Home Pay

The national average salary of $64,850 does not represent what a typical worker actually takes home. After federal and provincial income taxes, CPP contributions, and EI premiums, the take-home pay on $64,850 varies by province. For example:

  • Ontario: Approximately $51,200 net per year ($4,267 per month)
  • Alberta: Approximately $52,800 net per year ($4,400 per month)
  • Quebec: Approximately $48,400 net per year ($4,033 per month)
  • British Columbia: Approximately $51,600 net per year ($4,300 per month)

What Is Considered a Good Salary in Canada?

A good salary depends on where you live, your household size, and your financial goals. As a general guide, earning above the median salary ($40,500) means you earn more than half of Canadian workers. Earning above the average ($64,850) puts you in the upper portion of the income distribution. In expensive cities like Toronto and Vancouver, a salary of $70,000 to $80,000 is often considered necessary for a comfortable single-person lifestyle, while in smaller cities and rural areas, $50,000 can provide a similar standard of living.

Household income is often a more meaningful measure. The median household income in Canada is approximately $72,000, meaning that combined earnings from all household members place half of Canadian households above this threshold.

Salary Growth Trends

Average wages in Canada have grown by approximately 3 to 5 percent annually in recent years, driven by tight labour markets, minimum wage increases, and inflation. However, after adjusting for inflation (real wage growth), the gains have been more modest. The post-pandemic period (2022-2025) saw above-average nominal wage growth as employers competed for talent, particularly in technology, healthcare, and skilled trades. Looking ahead, wage growth is expected to moderate as inflation cools and the labour market reaches a more balanced state.

How to use an average salary figure

An average is a weak benchmark for a single negotiation and a strong one for a trend. Used to judge whether a specific offer is fair, it misleads in both directions: it is pulled upward by a small number of very high earners, and it flattens the differences between industries that matter far more than the national figure. Used to see whether wages in a sector are moving, over several years and from a consistent source, it is reliable.

The better comparison for an individual offer is the median for the same occupation in the same province, which Statistics Canada publishes for major occupational groups. It answers the question actually being asked, which is whether the employer is offering what the role typically pays, rather than how the role compares with every other job in the country.

Why province and industry outweigh the national figure

The provinces with the highest average wages are generally those with concentrated resource extraction or financial services, not those with the highest cost of living, and the two do not track each other. A salary that looks strong against the national average can be modest against local housing costs, and a salary that looks weak can go considerably further. Any comparison that ignores the cost side answers only half the question.

Industry effects are larger still. The gap between median pay in the highest-paying and lowest-paying sectors exceeds the gap between the highest and lowest provinces by a wide margin. For anyone weighing a career move, the sector matters more than the address, which the national average obscures entirely by collapsing both into one number.

Frequently asked questions

Is the average salary the same as the median?
No, and the difference matters. The average is pulled upward by very high earners, so it sits well above what a typical worker makes. The median, the point where half earn more and half less, is the better guide to whether a given offer is competitive. Most published comparisons quote the average because it is easier to obtain, which is why salaries so often look disappointing against it.
Why do salaries differ so much between provinces?
Industry mix explains most of it. Provinces with resource extraction or concentrated financial services carry higher averages, while those with more public sector and service employment sit lower. Cost of living follows only loosely: the province with the highest average wage is rarely the one where that wage goes furthest once housing is accounted for.
Does the average include part-time work?
It depends on the series. Figures for average weekly earnings from Statistics Canada cover all employees, including part-time, which drags the number down relative to full-time-only measures. Comparing an annual full-time salary against a series that includes part-time work is one of the most common errors in salary research, and it can distort the gap by several thousand dollars.
How current are these figures?
Statistics Canada publishes earnings data monthly with a lag of about two months, and annual income data from tax records with a lag closer to eighteen months. The figures on this page state their source and reference period. In periods of rapid wage growth a series eighteen months old understates current pay noticeably.
