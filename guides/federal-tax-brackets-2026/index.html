Federal tax brackets 2026: rates, thresholds, credits

Complete guide to the 2026 CRA federal income tax brackets with examples showing how progressive taxation works in Canada. Includes BPA and tax credits.

By Radif Partners, passionate about personal finance, Éditeur de calculateurs et de guides pratiques

Federal tax brackets apply to taxable income rather than to gross salary, and the gap between the two is where most confusion starts: registered pension and RRSP contributions, union dues and certain employment expenses come off before the brackets are applied. Each bracket taxes only the income inside it, so crossing a threshold changes the rate on the next dollar and on nothing else. The basic personal amount is itself income-tested at the top of the scale, which means the effective marginal rate in that range is slightly higher than the bracket rate suggests. Thresholds are indexed to inflation each year, so a figure without a year attached to it is unusable. The guide shows the computation for three incomes, line by line, so each step can be compared against a notice of assessment.

The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) adjusts federal tax brackets annually for inflation. For the 2026 tax year, Canada uses five progressive tax brackets, and the lowest rate is 14%, down from 15% before July 2025:

How Progressive Taxation Works

A common misconception is that earning more moves your entire income into a higher tax bracket. In reality, only the income within each bracket is taxed at that rate. This is called progressive or marginal taxation. The system works like a staircase: as your income climbs each step, only the income on that particular step is taxed at that step's rate. Your income on lower steps continues to be taxed at the lower rates, regardless of how much you earn in total.

For example, if you earn $80,000 in 2026:

  • The first $58,523 is taxed at 14% = $8,193
  • The remaining $21,477 ($80,000 – $58,523) is taxed at 20.5% = $4,403
  • Total federal tax before credits: $12,596

Your marginal rate is 20.5% (the rate on your last dollar), but your effective rate is only 15.7% ($12,596 divided by $80,000). This distinction is crucial for financial planning. When evaluating a raise, a bonus or an RRSP contribution, think in terms of your marginal rate. When budgeting or comparing your overall tax burden, the effective rate is more meaningful.

How Federal Tax Affects Different Income Levels

The progressive structure ensures that lower-income earners face a significantly lighter tax burden than higher earners. Here is how federal tax scales across income levels, before credits and after the basic personal amount, CPP and EI credits of an employee:

  • $40,000 income: federal tax before credits $5,600; after credits, about $2,901 (effective rate 7.3%).
  • $75,000 income: federal tax before credits $11,571; after credits, about $8,516 (effective rate 11.4%).
  • $120,000 income: federal tax before credits $20,959; after credits, about $17,848 (effective rate 14.9%).
  • $200,000 income: federal tax before credits $42,315; after credits, about $39,259 (effective rate 19.6%).
  • $300,000 income: federal tax before credits $72,976; after credits, about $70,092 (effective rate 23.4%).

The effective rate rises gradually from 7.3% at $40,000 to 23.4% at $300,000. Even at $300,000, the effective federal rate is well below the 33% top marginal rate, because the bulk of income is taxed at lower rates.

Basic Personal Amount (BPA)

The federal Basic Personal Amount for 2026 is $16,452. It provides a non-refundable tax credit at 14%, worth $2,303, effectively making the first $16,452 of income tax-free for most Canadians.

High-income earners (net income above $181,440) receive a reduced BPA. At $258,482 and above, the BPA is $14,829. Very high earners therefore pay slightly more federal tax on their first dollars of income.

Tax Calculation Example: $100,000 Salary

On a $100,000 salary (before deductions):

  • 14% on the first $58,523 = $8,193
  • 20.5% on $58,523 to $100,000 ($41,477) = $8,503
  • Gross federal tax: $16,696
  • BPA credit: $16,452 × 14% = $2,303
  • CPP credit: about $4,230 × 14%
  • EI credit: about $1,123 × 14%
  • Net federal tax: about $13,585

How Brackets Have Changed Over Time

Federal brackets are indexed annually to inflation using the Consumer Price Index (CPI). The 2026 thresholds are 2.0% higher than those of 2025, meaning you can earn slightly more before entering higher brackets. This prevents "bracket creep," where inflation-driven raises push you into higher tax brackets without increasing your real purchasing power.

Looking back over recent years, the indexation rates have varied: 6.3% for 2023 (reflecting high post-pandemic inflation), 4.7% for 2024, 2.7% for 2025 and 2.0% for 2026 as inflation moderated. The rates themselves had not changed since 2016, when the government added the fifth bracket at 33%. That changed in 2025: the lowest rate was cut from 15% to 14% from July 1, 2025, which gave a blended rate of 14.5% for 2025 and the full 14% for 2026.

For historical perspective, Canada introduced its modern income tax system in 1917 as a "temporary" wartime measure with rates ranging from 4% to 25%. The number of brackets has varied from as many as 16 in the 1980s to the current five, with significant simplification occurring in the tax reforms of 1987 and 2000.

How Federal Tax Interacts with Provincial Tax

Federal tax brackets are only part of your total income tax burden. Each province and territory sets its own brackets and rates, which are calculated separately and added to your federal tax. For example, a worker earning $100,000 in Ontario pays about $13,585 in net federal tax plus about $6,378 in Ontario provincial tax, for a combined income tax of about $19,963. The same worker in Alberta pays the same federal tax and about $6,954 in provincial tax, for a combined total of about $20,539.

Quebec is unique because it administers its own income tax separately (you file a separate Quebec return), and Quebec residents receive a 16.5% federal tax abatement. Their federal tax is reduced by 16.5%, but the Quebec provincial tax rates are significantly higher than those of other provinces.

Common Misconceptions

  • "A raise will push all my income into a higher bracket." False. Only the income above the bracket threshold is taxed at the higher rate. A raise always increases your after-tax income.
  • "The BPA means I do not pay tax on my first $16,452." Technically, the BPA is a non-refundable tax credit at 14%, not a deduction. The effect is similar, but the mechanism is different, and earners above $181,440 receive a reduced BPA.
  • "Capital gains are taxed at these rates." Capital gains receive preferential treatment in Canada: only half of a capital gain is included in taxable income, so the effective rate on a gain is about half the rate on employment income.
  • "Everyone pays 33% on high income." The 33% rate only applies to taxable income above $258,482. Someone earning $300,000 pays 33% only on the $41,518 above that threshold, not on their entire income.

Changes from 2025 to 2026: Specific Bracket Adjustments

The CRA applied a 2.0% indexation factor for 2026, based on the average Consumer Price Index over the twelve months ending September 2025. It is lower than the 2.7% used for 2025 and the 4.7% applied for 2024, reflecting the moderation of inflation.

The first bracket ceiling increased from $57,375 in 2025 to $58,523 in 2026, a rise of $1,148. The second threshold rose from $114,750 to $117,045, the third from $177,882 to $181,440, and the fourth from $253,414 to $258,482. A worker who received a 2% cost-of-living raise therefore stays in about the same bracket position. On top of that, the lowest rate applies at 14% for the whole of 2026, against a blended 14.5% in 2025: on $58,523 of taxable income, that half point is worth $293.

The Basic Personal Amount also increased with indexation. For 2026, the maximum BPA is $16,452, up from $16,129 in 2025. Because credits are valued at the lowest rate, the cut to 14% also reduces the value of every non-refundable credit: the BPA credit is $2,303.

Other notable changes for 2026 include the RRSP dollar limit ($33,810, up from $32,490), the TFSA annual contribution room ($7,000, unchanged), the CPP maximum pensionable earnings ($74,600, up from $71,300) and the EI maximum insurable earnings ($68,900, up from $65,700). Workers and financial planners should update their tax projections each January to account for these changes.

Practical Tax Planning Tips

  • Maximize RRSP contributions at high income. If your marginal rate is 26 percent or higher (income above $117,045), RRSP contributions provide substantial tax savings. A $10,000 contribution saves $2,600 to $3,300 in federal tax alone, plus provincial tax savings.
  • Use TFSA at lower income levels. If your income is below $58,523 (the 14% bracket), the RRSP deduction saves only 14% per dollar. A TFSA may be more valuable because withdrawals in retirement will be tax-free regardless of your future income.
  • Income splitting for couples. Pension income splitting allows retirees to shift up to 50 percent of eligible pension income to a lower-income spouse, potentially saving thousands in combined tax. Spousal RRSP contributions during working years achieve a similar result.
  • Time your income. If you expect a significantly different income next year (due to retirement, maternity leave, or a career change), consider shifting income or deductions across the year boundary to minimize total tax.

Key Takeaways

  • Canada has 5 federal tax brackets from 14% to 33% in 2026.
  • Only income within each bracket is taxed at that rate (progressive system).
  • The first $16,452 is effectively tax-free via the BPA credit.
  • Provincial tax is separate and additional (varies by province).
  • Brackets are indexed annually to inflation, preventing bracket creep.

Frequently asked questions

Does moving into a higher bracket reduce my take-home pay?
No. Each rate applies only to the portion of income within its bracket, so a raise never leaves you worse off. The confusion arises because the marginal rate, which applies to the next dollar, is always higher than the average rate paid across the whole salary. Benefits that phase out with income can create genuine cliffs, but the tax brackets themselves never do.
Are federal brackets the same in every province?
The federal brackets are identical across the country, but each province sets its own on top, with different rates and thresholds. Quebec is a special case: it collects its own income tax and residents receive a federal abatement in exchange. The combined rate therefore varies by several points depending on where you live, on exactly the same salary.
What is the basic personal amount?
It is the slice of income on which no federal tax is payable, delivered as a non-refundable credit rather than a deduction. It is reduced gradually for high earners and is indexed each January. Each province has its own equivalent at a different level, which is why two people with the same gross income in different provinces start paying tax at different points.
When are the brackets updated?
Thresholds are indexed to inflation and take effect on the first of January each year, based on the change in the Consumer Price Index over the twelve months to September. Rates themselves change only through a budget measure, which is far less frequent. A page quoting a threshold from an earlier year will be wrong by a few hundred dollars rather than by a whole bracket.
